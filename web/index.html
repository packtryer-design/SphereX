    What it's for

    Volunteers discovering new worlds

    In 2017 a science teacher in Tasmania found a new brown dwarf by flipping between NASA WISE images in the Backyard Worlds project; volunteers have since found many more. SpotX gives anyone the same flipbook for SPHEREx's brand-new data, plus a spectrum to check a find.

    Hunting faint moving objects

    Shift-and-stack is how astronomers look for distant planets and faint asteroids that no single image shows. The Planet X page runs it on real SPHEREx frames: it finds Pluto blind and recovers a planted test planet.

    A real lab for classrooms

    Students measure how far Barnard's Star moved between this year's visits and work out its speed, with today's space-telescope data instead of 1950s photographic plates.

    Observations

    Is a difference real?

    Before reading a change between two visits as something happening in space, rule out these first:

    • Different detectors. SPHEREx has six detectors, each covering a different range of infrared colours. If two visits don't use the same detectors, they show the object in different colours. SpotX warns you when that happens, and its Difference view only subtracts detectors both visits share.
    • Noise. Faint, grainy differences that don't form a shape are usually noise. A real change keeps its shape from one visit to the next.
    • Partial visits. Some visits caught only part of the field, or used fewer exposures. They are noisier and can have blank edges.
    • Processing. All visits share one alignment grid and one brightness scale, so one visit can't look brighter just because it was scaled differently. Bright stars can still leave artifacts, such as ghosts and spikes, that differ between visits.

    Things that really do change over months: nearby stars moving across the sky, variable stars, and asteroids or comets passing through. A nebula's gas is light-years across, so it won't visibly change between visits.
